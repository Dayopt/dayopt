# Best Timeboxing Apps in 2026: Features, Pricing, and Comparison

[Timeboxing](/blog/timeboxing-guide) is one of the simplest productivity techniques out there: assign a fixed time slot to a task, work on it, stop when the timer ends. But the app you use changes the experience dramatically.

Some tools are built for timeboxing from the ground up. Others are general-purpose calendars or task managers that you can adapt. This guide compares seven popular options so you can pick the one that fits your workflow — not just the one with the best marketing.

## What to Look for in a Timeboxing App

Before diving into specific tools, here's what actually matters for timeboxing. Not every app needs all of these — but the more it covers, the less friction you'll face when building the habit.

**Calendar integration.** Timeboxing lives on your calendar. If the app doesn't sync with Google Calendar, Outlook, or Apple Calendar, you'll end up maintaining two systems. That doubles your work and halves your compliance. The ideal setup shows your timeboxes alongside meetings in a single view.

**Plan-to-record workflow.** The real power of timeboxing isn't the planning — it's comparing your plan to what actually happened. Apps that track planned versus actual time help you get better at estimating over time. Without this feedback loop, you're just creating calendar events and hoping for the best.

**Keyboard-first interaction.** Creating and adjusting timeboxes should take seconds, not minutes. If you have to click through three menus to create a 30-minute block, you'll stop doing it within a week. The best tools let you create, resize, move, and complete timeboxes from the keyboard alone.

**Recurring schedules.** Most of your week follows patterns. The app should support recurring timeboxes so you're not rebuilding your schedule from scratch every Monday. Morning planning, email triage, weekly reviews — these should be set-and-forget.

**Cross-platform access.** Your plan needs to be available wherever you are — desktop, phone, tablet. A tool that only works on one platform creates gaps in your workflow.

**Visual clarity.** You should be able to glance at your day and instantly understand its structure. Color-coding, clear time labels, and a clean layout make the difference between a calendar you check and one you ignore.

| Feature                     | Why It Matters                      |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Calendar sync               | Keeps timeboxes alongside meetings  |
| Planned vs. actual tracking | Builds estimation accuracy          |
| Keyboard shortcuts          | Reduces friction for daily use      |
| Recurring timeboxes         | Automates routine scheduling        |
| Drag-and-drop               | Quick adjustments when plans change |
| Mobile access               | Check your schedule anywhere        |

## Dayopt

Dayopt is purpose-built for timeboxing. While most other tools on this list are calendar apps, task managers, or project tools that can be adapted for timeboxing, Dayopt was designed around the plan-execute-review cycle from day one.

**How timeboxing works in Dayopt:** Press `N` to create a Plan, drag it onto your calendar, and set the duration. When you finish (or time runs out), mark it complete — Dayopt creates a Record that captures the actual time spent. Over days and weeks, you see exactly where your estimates were right and where they were off.

**Key strengths:**

- **Plan + Record workflow.** Every timebox has a planned version and an actual version. This is the core of effective timeboxing, and most other tools don't support it natively.
- **Built-in time tracking.** No need for a separate tool like Toggl or Clockify. Start a timer on any Plan and it becomes a Record automatically.
- **Keyboard-first design.** Create, move, resize, and complete timeboxes without touching the mouse. Power users can manage their entire day from the keyboard.
- **Calendar integration.** Syncs with Google Calendar so your timeboxes sit alongside meetings and appointments.

**Pros:**

- Purpose-built for timeboxing, not adapted from another use case
- Plan vs. actual comparison is automatic
- Fast keyboard-driven workflow

**Cons:**

- Smaller ecosystem compared to Google or Microsoft tools
- Mobile app is newer than desktop experience

**Pricing:** $5/month after a 45-day free trial that starts when you first open the app after signing in. Every feature is included during the trial and subscription.

**Best for:** Anyone who wants timeboxing as their primary productivity method, not just a feature inside a larger tool. Pairs well with a [timeboxing template](/blog/timeboxing-template) for getting started.

**Bottom line:** If you've read our [timeboxing guide](/blog/timeboxing-guide) and want a tool that mirrors the plan-execute-review workflow described there, Dayopt is the closest match.

## Google Calendar

Google Calendar isn't a timeboxing app. It's a calendar. But because timeboxing is fundamentally about putting tasks on a calendar, Google Calendar works surprisingly well — especially if you already live in the Google ecosystem.

**How timeboxing works in Google Calendar:** Create events for your tasks. Give each event a title, start time, and end time. Use color-coding to distinguish task categories. When the event ends, you move on.

**Pros:**

- Free and available everywhere
- Excellent mobile apps
- Deep integration with Gmail, Meet, and Google Workspace

**Cons:**

- No plan-vs-actual tracking — once the event passes, there's no record of what happened
- No task-specific features (priorities, completion status)
- Color-coding is limited to a fixed palette

**Pricing:** Free.

**Best for:** People who want to try timeboxing without installing anything new. If you find yourself wanting more structure — like tracking how long tasks actually took versus how long you planned — it's a sign you've outgrown it and should consider a purpose-built tool.

## Sunsama

Sunsama markets itself as "the daily planner for busy professionals." It combines task management with calendar blocking in a guided daily planning ritual.

**How timeboxing works in Sunsama:** Each morning, Sunsama walks you through a planning flow: pull in tasks from Asana, Trello, Jira, or email, then drag them onto your calendar with time estimates. At the end of the day, it prompts a shutdown ritual.

**Pros:**

- Beautiful, calming interface
- Guided planning reduces decision fatigue
- Integrates with many project management tools

**Cons:**

- $20/month with no free tier — expensive for individual users
- The guided ritual can feel slow if you prefer to plan quickly
- Limited customization for power users

**Pricing:** $20/month (14-day free trial).

**Best for:** People who need structure and a daily ritual to stay consistent with timeboxing.

## Akiflow

Akiflow positions itself as a "command center" that consolidates tasks from everywhere — email, Slack, project tools — into a single calendar view.

**How timeboxing works in Akiflow:** Tasks arrive in a universal inbox. You triage them, assign time estimates, and drag them onto your calendar. Akiflow blocks the time automatically and syncs back to your calendar.

**Pros:**

- Universal inbox captures tasks from many sources
- Fast keyboard shortcuts
- Good calendar integration

**Cons:**

- $24.99/month — the most expensive option on this list
- Complexity can be overkill if you just want simple timeboxing
- Steeper learning curve

**Pricing:** $24.99/month (7-day free trial).

**Best for:** Power users who manage tasks across many tools and need a single place to timebox everything.

## Reclaim.ai

Reclaim.ai uses AI to automatically find time for your tasks, habits, and meetings. Instead of manually dragging tasks onto your calendar, you tell Reclaim what you need to do and how long it takes, and it finds open slots.

**How timeboxing works in Reclaim.ai:** Add tasks with time estimates and priority levels. Reclaim scans your calendar for openings and schedules them automatically. If a meeting gets added, Reclaim rearranges your tasks around it.

**Pros:**

- AI scheduling saves planning time
- Habit scheduling for recurring activities
- Integrates with Google Calendar and project tools

**Cons:**

- Less control over exactly when tasks are scheduled — the AI decides
- Only works with Google Calendar (no Outlook or Apple Calendar)
- Free tier is limited; meaningful features require paid plans

**Pricing:** Free tier available. Paid plans from $10-15/month.

**Best for:** People with unpredictable calendars who want the computer to handle scheduling. Less ideal if you want full control over your timeboxes.

## Todoist + Calendar

Todoist is a task manager, not a calendar app. But it has solid calendar integration that lets you assign due dates and times to tasks, then view them on Google Calendar or Outlook.

**How timeboxing works with Todoist:** Create tasks with due dates and durations. Sync with your calendar app. Tasks appear as events. When you complete a task, it disappears from the calendar.

**Pros:**

- Excellent task management with projects, labels, and filters
- Natural language input ("Write report tomorrow 2pm for 1 hour")
- Free tier is generous

**Cons:**

- Timeboxing is a workaround, not a core feature
- Calendar sync can be laggy
- No planned-vs-actual time tracking

**Pricing:** Free tier available. Pro plan at $5/month.

**Best for:** People who already use Todoist and want to add lightweight timeboxing without switching tools.

## Notion

Notion is the Swiss Army knife of productivity. You can build almost anything in it — including a timeboxing system using databases, calendar views, and templates.

**How timeboxing works in Notion:** Create a database with task name, start time, end time, status, and category fields. Use the calendar view to see your timeboxes. Build templates for recurring blocks.

**Pros:**

- Extremely flexible — build exactly the system you want
- Combines notes, tasks, and calendar in one workspace
- Good free tier for individuals

**Cons:**

- You have to build the timeboxing system yourself — Notion provides the building blocks, not the solution
- Performance can lag with large databases
- Mobile experience is mediocre for quick timeboxing

**Pricing:** Free tier available. Plus plan at $10/month.

**Best for:** People who love customization and want timeboxing embedded in a larger knowledge management system.

## Verdict: Which App Is Right for You?

There's no single best app. The right choice depends on what you value most.

**If you want purpose-built timeboxing:** Try Dayopt. It's the only tool on this list designed specifically around the timebox-execute-review cycle. The Plan + Record workflow and built-in time tracking mean you don't need to bolt together multiple tools. Start with [5 practical timeboxing tips with Dayopt](/blog/timeboxing-with-dayopt).

**If you want zero cost and simplicity:** Start with Google Calendar. It won't track your actual time, but it's free, fast, and available everywhere.

**If you want a guided daily ritual:** Sunsama's morning planning flow and evening shutdown are great for building consistency — if you're willing to pay $20/month.

**If you manage tasks across many tools:** Akiflow's universal inbox makes sense when your tasks live in Slack, email, Jira, and Asana simultaneously.

**If you want AI to schedule for you:** Reclaim.ai removes the manual work of finding time slots — at the cost of some control.

**If you're already a Todoist user:** Adding calendar sync is the lowest-friction way to start timeboxing without changing your workflow.

**If you love building custom systems:** Notion lets you design exactly the timeboxing setup you want — but you have to build it yourself.

**A note on pricing:** Free tools work fine for getting started. But if timeboxing becomes your core productivity system, a paid tool that saves you 30 minutes of daily planning overhead pays for itself many times over. The real cost isn't the subscription — it's the time lost to a tool that doesn't fit your workflow.

The most important thing isn't which app you pick. It's whether you actually use it consistently. Start with one tool, give it two weeks, and adjust from there.
